Nigeria's Power Sector: A 60-Year Story

For over six decades, Nigeria's power sector has struggled to deliver reliable, affordable and sustainable electricity. Despite vast resources and repeated reforms, millions of people and businesses still face unreliable supply, high energy costs and heavy dependence on diesel generators.

This has constrained economic growth, limited industrial competitiveness and affected the quality of life for households across the country.

A Brief History

How Nigeria's energy story has evolved, and where Energy Metrics Africa fits in.

1898

First Generating Plant: Lagos

Nigeria's first electricity generating plant is installed in Lagos by the colonial administration, serving only government offices and wealthy households.

Scattered, uncoordinated private and colonial undertakings follow over the next five decades, with no national grid or regulatory structure connecting them, a pattern that persists well into independence.

1972

NEPA Is Formed

The Electricity Corporation of Nigeria (1951) and the Niger Dam Authority (1962) merge into NEPA, a single monopoly over generation, transmission and distribution.

Despite an initial capacity of roughly 6,200MW, NEPA becomes synonymous with chronic outages and institutional inefficiency. Nigerians rebrand the acronym: “Never Expect Power Always.”

2005

Reform & Privatisation

The Electric Power Sector Reform Act creates NERC as an independent regulator and unbundles NEPA. Full privatisation follows in 2013, handing generation and distribution to private investors.

Six GenCos, eleven DisCos and the Transmission Company of Nigeria (TCN) emerge from the breakup. Private ownership was expected to catalyse investment and efficiency, results that remain inconsistent over a decade later.

2023

Electricity Act 2023

Nigeria's most significant electricity legislation since 2005 decentralises the market, empowering states to run independent electricity markets and strengthening the legal framework for mini-grids and embedded generation.

The shift builds on REA's 2016 Mini-Grid Regulations and the World Bank's DARES programme, which had already begun channelling development finance into distributed renewables, opening the door for companies like Energy Metrics Africa to scale.

Our Core Values

The engineering and ethical principles governing every decision, from residential systems to community engagement in rural Northern Nigeria.

01

Engineering Integrity

Every system to IEC 60364 and BS standards. Every drawing by a certified engineer. Every installation tested before handover. We never compromise: lives and livelihoods depend on the systems we build.

02

Data Before Decisions

We never guess when we can measure. IoT monitoring and analytics are the foundation of everything we do. If we cannot measure impact, we will not claim it.

03

Community Partnership

Mini-grids succeed when communities own them emotionally. We embed local operators, train energy champions, and build relationships before laying a single cable.

04

Climate Accountability

Every solar kilowatt is a litre of diesel not burned. We track and publish carbon displacement metrics on every project, aligned with Nigeria's NDC under the Paris Agreement.

05

Commercial Discipline

Impact and revenue are not in conflict. PAYG and subscription models create sustainability that outlasts any grant cycle. Commercial discipline makes our mission achievable at scale.

06

Africa-First Innovation

We build for Nigerian realities: FX volatility, supply chain complexity, regulatory challenges. Local insight earned through real project experience is our most valuable competitive advantage.
